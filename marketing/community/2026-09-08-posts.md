Sustained high-load uses a decay counter rather than a hard reset — a single non-high message decrements by 1 instead of resetting to zero, so a brief correct answer doesn't wipe the history of sustained struggle.

Layered above that: LOOM (arxiv:2511.21037) builds a knowledge graph per student, tracking concept mastery with FSRS time-decay applied to mastery scores so that memory of a concept fades if not reinforced. LECTOR (arxiv:2508.03275) uses that graph to reorder the review queue — prerequisites before dependents, confusion pairs clustered for contrast review, proactive interference boosts for high-weight confused-concept pairs before the student makes the mistake. ## b) r/LocalLLaMA

**Title:** `Built FSRS-decayed mastery tracking on a knowledge graph for local AI tutor (Ollama, llama3.2:3b, MIT)`

---

Been building an open-source local AI tutor (OpenTutor) that uses Ollama by default and wanted to share how we hooked the LOOM knowledge graph into FSRS retrievability for concept mastery tracking — something you don't usually see in local AI projects.

The problem: flashcard FSRS tells you when to review a *card*, but it doesn't know that "Chain Rule" is a prerequisite for "Integration by Parts". If you forget Chain Rule and FSRS hasn't scheduled it yet, the tutor will happily keep drilling you on integration and wonder why you're struggling.

The solution is in `loom_graph.py` (428 lines). When computing the mastery graph for display or review planning, it applies FSRS retrievability decay to each concept's mastery score directly:

```python
# Apply FSRS retrievability decay: mastery fades over time
if m and m.last_practiced_at and m.stability_days and m.stability_days > 0:
    elapsed = (now - last).total_seconds() / 86400
    from services.spaced_repetition.fsrs import _retrievability
    retrievability = _retrievability(elapsed, m.stability_days)
    mastery = raw_mastery * retrievability  # mastery decays like memory
```

So if you practiced Chain Rule 14 days ago but its stability is only 7 days, its mastery score shows up at ~50% of what you last earned — even before you review again. The knowledge graph node for "Integration by Parts" then correctly reports a weak prerequisite and surfaces it at the top of your review queue.

The LECTOR review scorer (`lector.py`, 344 lines) adds priority factors on top: low mastery gets a boost, never-practiced concepts get a larger one, prerequisites with mastery below threshold trigger a boost on the dependent concept, and concepts that appear in `confused_with` edges get proactively surfaced before you make the mistake rather than after. The interference factor only fires when edge weight > 0.6 — tuned to avoid over-triggering on vague semantic similarity.

Runs with `LLM_PROVIDER=ollama` and `LLM_MODEL=llama3.2:3b` by default. The knowledge graph extraction and mastery updates happen locally; the graph queries are async SQLAlchemy against SQLite. No cloud. Docker Compose one-liner to start. MIT. https://github.com/zijinz456/OpenTutor

## c) r/Anki

**Title:** `What if Anki's review order knew that you forgot a prerequisite? LOOM+LECTOR does this for free`

---

One thing FSRS doesn't do on its own — and Anki can't do currently — is change the *ordering* of your review queue based on concept dependencies. FSRS tells you *when* to review. LECTOR tells you *what to review first* given what else you're weak on.

OpenTutor (MIT, self-hosted) implements LECTOR (arxiv:2508.03275) on top of FSRS-5/6. Here's what it adds:

**Prerequisite ordering.** When the knowledge graph extraction runs on your material, it builds edges typed `prerequisite`, `related`, and `confused_with`. During review scheduling, if concept A is a prerequisite for concept B, and A's mastery is below threshold (default: 0.5), then B gets its priority score boosted — not because B itself is weak, but because practicing B without understanding A is wasted effort. You'll see A surface first.

**Confusion pair contrast review.** When two concepts share a `confused_with` edge (e.g., "sympathetic" vs. "parasympathetic" nervous system, "mitosis" vs. "meiosis"), they get clustered for co-review. Studying them back-to-back with explicit contrast is better than reviewing them days apart when the interference isn't visible.

**Proactive interference detection.** LECTOR goes one step further: if a `confused_with` edge has weight > 0.6, the boost fires *regardless* of whether the confused concept is currently weak. The algorithm surfaces the pair before you've had a chance to confuse them, not just after you get one wrong.

**Mastery fades like memory.** The LOOM graph applies FSRS retrievability decay to mastery scores directly, so `mastery = raw_mastery * R(elapsed, stability)`. A concept you haven't touched in 2× its stability interval shows up as genuinely weak in the graph — not just in your flashcard queue.

How does it compare to Anki? Anki's built-in FSRS handles *scheduling* very well. It does not handle *semantic relationships* between cards at all — every card is an island. LECTOR uses those relationships to change priority ordering without replacing FSRS. The two are complementary: FSRS decides the interval, LECTOR decides which due card to show you first when multiple are due. Codebase is MIT, runs locally, no cloud. https://github.com/zijinz456/OpenTutor

## d) r/GetStudying / r/medicalschool

**Title:** `I was failing physiology practice questions even after reviewing — the problem wasn't what I thought`

---

I was putting in 3+ hours a day on physiology before my shelf exam and still blanking on questions about cardiac output and preload/afterload. Reviewed the material twice. Could explain the concepts individually. Still got the practice questions wrong.

The issue was prerequisites. I had gaps in the underlying pressure-volume relationships that I'd marked as "understood" weeks earlier but hadn't touched since. On its own each concept felt fine. When I needed to chain them — "what happens to stroke volume when afterload increases and heart rate compensates" — I was rebuilding from fragments in real time instead of recalling something solid.

What finally helped: instead of treating each concept as a separate thing to review, I mapped out the dependency chain and checked whether every upstream concept was actually solid, not just vaguely familiar. That meant some very unsexy flashcard sessions on stuff I thought I knew, before touching the integrated clinical reasoning questions at all.

The uncomfortable truth is that "I understand this" after one review session usually means recognition, not recall. You can see a concept and feel like you know it and still completely fail to reconstruct it under exam conditions. The only test that works is closing everything and generating an explanation from scratch — if you're reaching for words or noticing gaps mid-sentence, that's exactly what needs to be reviewed before the downstream material.

I'm now using a local open-source tool (OpenTutor, free, no cloud) that automates the prerequisite tracking. It builds a dependency graph from your material, tracks mastery per concept with time-decay so things you haven't touched actually show as weak, and surfaces the upstream gaps before you even get to the downstream material that depends on them. Saved me a lot of time re-reviewing things I'd already mastered instead of drilling the specific links I'd let fade.

For anyone else hitting the "I reviewed this twice and still blank" wall — the likely answer is a prerequisite gap, not insufficient total study time.

## e) Dev.to / Hashnode — Technical Article

**Title:** How I built a 12-signal cognitive load detector that adapts my AI tutor's prompts in real time

---

The canonical failure mode for AI tutors: a student is overwhelmed, makes three consecutive wrong answers, sends terse one-word replies, and the AI cheerfully proceeds to the next harder concept. It cannot tell the difference between "I need a break" and "I'm ready for more."

For OpenTutor (open-source local-first learning platform, MIT) we built a real-time cognitive load detector that runs on every incoming message without a separate inference call and injects a guidance fragment into the tutor's system prompt. Here's the architecture.

### Why behavioral signals instead of explicit feedback

Explicit feedback ("how are you feeling?") interrupts flow and gets gamed. Students click through confirmations to get to the content. Behavioral signals are always-on and honest: a student who's frustrated sends shorter messages, takes longer between responses, and makes more mistakes — regardless of what they report when asked.

The research basis is "Cognitive Load Theory meets Deep Knowledge Tracing" (Nature Scientific Reports, 2025, doi:10.1038/s41598-025-10497-x) which demonstrates that combining behavioral signals with knowledge tracing significantly improves difficulty adaptation accuracy. Our implementation is in `services/cognitive_load.py` (488 lines).

### The 12-signal architecture

Each signal produces a 0.0–1.0 value. A weighted sum gives the final load score, also 0.0–1.0. Signals:

```python
async def compute_cognitive_load(
    db: AsyncSession,
    user_id: uuid.UUID,
    course_id: uuid.UUID,
    fatigue_score: float = 0.0,      # pre-computed from text patterns
    session_messages: int = 0,
    user_message: str = "",
) -> dict:
    """Returns score, level (low/medium/high), guidance str, and signal breakdown."""
    signals: dict[str, float] = {}
    load = 0.0

    # Signal 1: Text fatigue (pre-computed word-pattern match)
    signals["fatigue"] = fatigue_score
    load += fatigue_score * settings.cognitive_load_weight_fatigue

    # Signal 2: Session length — degrades after ~45 min
    session_fatigue = min(session_messages / settings.cognitive_load_session_messages_norm, 1.0)
    load += session_fatigue * settings.cognitive_load_weight_session_length

    # Signal 3: Unmastered wrong answer count (DB query)
    # Signal 4: Message brevity — short messages correlate with frustration
    # Signal 5: Help-seeking keywords ("confused", "stuck", "how do i")
    # Signal 6: Recent quiz accuracy vs. target accuracy
    # Signal 7: Answer hesitation — median response time over last 10 answers
    # Signal 8: NLP affect analysis (frustration + confusion scores)
    # Signal 9: Relative baseline calibration (per-user, persisted)
    # Signal 10: Wrong-answer streak (3+ consecutive wrong = high signal)
    # Signal 11: Message gap timing (30s–300s range triggers signal)
    # Signal 12: Repeated errors on same problem (deep confusion indicator)

    return {"score": score, "level": level, "guidance": guidance_str, "signals": signals}
```

A few design decisions worth explaining:

**Signal 8 (NLP affect) runs every 3 messages, cached in between.** An LLM call for affect analysis costs ~500ms per invocation. Running it on every message would make the tutor feel slow. Running it every third message and reusing the last result for intermediate messages keeps detection latency negligible on the hot path.

```python
_should_run_nlp = (session_messages <= 1) or (session_messages % 3 == 0)
if _should_run_nlp and user_message:
    affect = await analyze_student_affect(user_message)
    async with _last_affect_lock:
        _last_affect[str(user_id)] = affect
else:
    async with _last_affect_lock:
        affect = _last_affect.get(str(user_id), {})

frustration = affect.get("frustration", 0.0)
confusion = affect.get("confusion", 0.0)
nlp_signal = (frustration * settings.cognitive_load_nlp_frustration_weight
              + confusion * settings.cognitive_load_nlp_confusion_weight)
```

The cache is bounded at 200 entries (`_MAX_TRACKING_SIZE`) with LRU eviction to prevent unbounded memory growth in long sessions.

**Signal 10 uses a decay counter, not a hard reset.** Early version reset the consecutive-high counter to zero on any non-high message. That meant a single correct answer erased evidence of sustained struggle. The fix: on a non-high message, decrement by 1 instead of resetting. Three high-load messages in a row followed by one normal message still reads as "pattern of struggle" rather than "student recovered."

```python
if level == "high":
    _consecutive_high[key] = _consecutive_high.get(key, 0) + 1
    if _consecutive_high[key] >= settings.cognitive_load_intervention_threshold:
        guidance += " [Proactive intervention: student showing sustained high load]"
else:
    # Decay rather than reset — one good message shouldn't erase the streak
    _consecutive_high[key] = max(0, _consecutive_high.get(key, 0) - 1)
```

### Injecting load into the tutor's system prompt

The load score feeds a guidance string that gets appended to the tutor's system prompt at call time:

- `low`: No guidance injected.
- `medium`: "Student may be experiencing moderate difficulty. Prefer shorter explanations and check understanding before proceeding."
- `high`: "Student appears cognitively overloaded. Simplify significantly, break into smaller steps, offer to revisit prerequisites."
- Sustained high (consecutive threshold crossed): "Proactive intervention: student showing sustained high load — consider suggesting a break or switching to easier review material."

The tutor doesn't see the raw score, just the natural-language guidance. This keeps the injection small (one sentence) and avoids over-constraining the LLM's response style.

### Connecting to the knowledge graph

Cognitive load detection runs in parallel with LOOM/LECTOR's prerequisite tracking. A student who's overloaded on "Integration by Parts" might have a weak prerequisite in "Chain Rule" — and LECTOR surfaces that prerequisite first, which is usually a faster path to resolution than repeating the harder concept at a lower difficulty. The two systems don't directly call each other: load detection informs *how* the tutor explains, LECTOR informs *what* the tutor focuses on.

### What this doesn't do well yet

Per-user signal weights are currently the same defaults across all students. The roadmap item "Cognitive load weight auto-tuning" would personalize the weights from each student's response-to-load patterns over time. The data collection hooks are in (`cognitive_load_tuning.py`) but the optimizer isn't wired yet — this is the next thing we're building.
