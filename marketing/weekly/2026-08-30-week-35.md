## Reddit Post — r/LocalLLaMA

**Title:** How I fixed cognitive load signal saturation in my local LLM tutor — 136-line sliding window auto-tuner, no ML

**Body:**

OpenTutor is an open-source, local-first AI learning tool — FastAPI + Next.js + SQLite, runs with Ollama or any OpenAI-compatible endpoint. One of its features is real-time cognitive load detection: after each student message, the system computes a load score from 6 behavioral signals and uses it to decide whether the tutor should simplify, slow down, or suggest a break.

The signals: error rate, response latency, question complexity, conversation depth, session duration, and affect. Each gets a configured weight. The score is a weighted sum normalized to [0, 1]. Above 0.7 is "high load" and triggers an intervention; above 0.85 triggers a more direct one.

The problem I ran into: fixed weights don't account for per-student baseline variation. Some students consistently produce high error rates — not because they're overwhelmed, but because they're studying genuinely hard material at the edge of their knowledge. After 20 messages, their error_rate is near 1.0 every session. The original implementation treated this the same as acute overload. The score saturated and stayed there, drowning out signals that were actually changing. The system was permanently alarmed and therefore useless as an alarm.

The fix I shipped (April 2, writing now) is `services/cognitive_load_tuning.py`, a 136-line sliding window auto-tuner. After each `compute_cognitive_load()` call, `update_and_get_multipliers()` records the signal value in a per-user `collections.deque(maxlen=20)`. Once there are at least 5 data points:

- If the 20-message rolling mean for a signal is ≥ 0.65 (`HIGH_SIGNAL_THRESHOLD`), the weight multiplier decays: `new = max(0.40, current × 0.90)`
- If the mean drops below 0.65, the multiplier recovers: `new = min(1.0, current × 1.04)`

The next `compute_cognitive_load()` call applies these multipliers to the config weights before computing the score. A signal that's been persistently high for 20+ messages contributes progressively less, making room for signals that are actually varying to dominate.

The constants are chosen by inspection, not optimization. I looked at signal traces from a few sessions and asked "at what point does this signal stop being informative?" That's not a methodology. The right values likely differ by domain — math practice vs. language learning vs. medical flashcards probably have different baseline error rate distributions — and I don't have enough cross-domain session data to find out.

Known limitation: state is in-memory, bounded to 200 users by a least-active eviction policy. Multipliers reset on server restart. A student who studies across two server restarts the same day re-accumulates baseline from scratch. Persisting to SQLite is on the list for Phase 2.

Two other things in the same commit: a locked block overlay in the UI (OpenTutor progressively unlocks 12 composable learning blocks; now locked blocks show the lock icon and the specific unlock reason), and a new ambient monitor action — `propose_content_generation` — that can suggest generating notes or a quiz but only creates a `pending_approval` task. The student must explicitly approve before anything runs.

OpenTutor is MIT. The full implementation is in `services/cognitive_load.py` and `services/cognitive_load_tuning.py`. One open question I haven't solved: for the cross-session baseline persistence problem, I'm choosing between a Redis TTL key (simple, new dependency) or a SQLite table with per-user signal history (no new dependency, adds write overhead on every load computation). Has anyone solved per-user signal normalization for adaptive educational systems in a way that doesn't require a persistent store?

## Reddit Post — r/Anki

**Title:** My AI tutor's cognitive load score was permanently at 0.9 — here's why that breaks adaptive learning and what I did about it

**Body:**

I've been building OpenTutor (open-source, local-first, runs on Ollama), an AI-assisted study tool with FSRS 4.5 scheduling. One feature is real-time cognitive load detection — after each student message, the system computes a load score from behavioral signals like error rate and response time, and uses it to adjust the tutor's behavior.

This is conceptually similar to FSRS's difficulty and stability estimates. In Anki, FSRS uses self-reported difficulty ratings to calibrate how quickly your memory trace decays for each card. In OpenTutor, the system tries to infer session-level difficulty automatically from behavioral signals rather than asking you to rate every interaction. The goal is the same: match the task to the learner's current state.

The problem I hit: some students have chronically high error rates. They're studying at the edge of their knowledge — the Zone of Proximal Development, which is exactly where they should be — but my cognitive load formula treated persistent high error rate as a signal of overload. The score would sit at 0.85 from the first message and stay there. The system kept triggering interventions — "let's take this slower," "should we review a prerequisite?" — even when the student was engaging and making progress. After a while, they learned to ignore the interventions.

The analog in Anki: imagine if FSRS rated every card where you pressed "Hard" as a signal of poor retention, regardless of whether hard was your baseline rating style or a genuine spike. Some users rate almost everything hard — not because their retention is poor, but because their threshold for "easy" is high. FSRS partially handles this through per-card calibration; the system learns that this user's hard ratings correspond to a different stability than another user's hard ratings. My cognitive load system had no equivalent per-user calibration.

The fix is a sliding window auto-tuner: `cognitive_load_tuning.py`, 136 lines. For each signal, I maintain a rolling 20-message mean per user. When a signal's mean exceeds 0.65 for long enough, its weight multiplier decays (×0.90 per update, floor at 0.40). When the mean drops, it recovers (×1.04). A persistently high signal gradually loses its ability to dominate the score, making room for signals that are actually changing — like a sudden spike in response latency or a jump in question complexity.

The connection to spaced repetition: FSRS tracks memory stability per card; this tuner tracks "signal credibility" per signal per user. Both are trying to avoid conflating chronic patterns with acute events. The difference is FSRS has millions of reviews to calibrate against. My tuner has a 20-message window per session. Data-sparse, obviously.

What I don't know: whether adaptive signal weights actually improve intervention timing compared to a simpler approach like just capping the max score per user. I'd need to compare intervention precision across a population with and without the tuner, and I don't have that data. Has anyone built automated cognitive load estimation for conversational tutors? I'm curious whether behavioral signal normalization is a known-solved problem in educational data mining, or whether everyone is still working around it with simpler heuristics.

## IndieHackers Post

**Title:** My cognitive load detector was permanently alarmed. 136 lines to make it useful again.

**Body:**

OpenTutor is a local-first AI learning tool I've been building for about a year. One of the pieces I'm most uncertain about — and most invested in — is real-time cognitive load detection. As a student studies, the system watches behavioral signals and computes a score from 0 to 1. When the score crosses 0.7, the tutor adjusts: shorter explanations, simpler language, fewer new concepts per turn.

The idea comes from Cognitive Load Theory: working memory has a finite capacity, and effective instruction keeps total load within that capacity. The original design goal was a tutor that could sense overload the way a good human tutor would — pulling back when a student was struggling, not just plowing ahead through the material.

The problem I noticed after watching session recordings: some students scored 0.85 from the very first message. Every session. The system was triggering overload interventions immediately and staying in that mode permanently. The students weren't overwhelmed — they were asking good questions, making progress, engaging with hard material. The detector was lying.

The root cause: error rate carried too much weight, and some students have chronically high error rates. They're working at the edge of their knowledge — actually the right place to study — but my formula treated "wrong answer" the same regardless of whether it was a pattern or a spike. A student who consistently gets things wrong because they're studying genuinely hard material looked identical to a student who suddenly hit a concept that exceeded their current knowledge. The formula had no way to distinguish them.

The fix is 136 lines: `services/cognitive_load_tuning.py`. It's a per-user sliding window over the last 20 cognitive load computations. When a signal's rolling mean stays above 0.65, the tuner dampens its weight: the multiplier decays by 10% per update, down to a floor of 40% of the original. When the mean drops, the weight slowly recovers. A persistently high signal gradually contributes less, making room for signals that are actually changing.

The honest part: the constants are educated guesses — WINDOW_SIZE=20, threshold=0.65, decay=0.90, floor=0.40. I chose them by looking at three session traces and asking "at what point does this stop being informative?" That's not rigorous. The right values probably vary by subject domain. And there's a genuine failure mode I haven't solved: a student who is chronically overloaded (not just high-baseline) will also have their error_rate weight dampened over time, making the system less responsive to their actual struggle. I don't have a way to distinguish "always wrong because studying hard" from "always wrong because actually overwhelmed" without additional signals.

The same commit added two other things I'd been putting off: a locked overlay on the block UI — you can now see which of OpenTutor's 12 composable learning blocks are locked and the specific reason each one hasn't unlocked yet — and a new ambient monitor action that can propose generating notes or a quiz, but only queues it as a pending approval task. The student has to explicitly confirm before anything runs. Both were simpler to ship than the tuning work but had been sitting in the backlog for weeks.

OpenTutor is at github.com/zijinz456/opentutor. What's your model for distinguishing "persistently high" from "acutely high" in adaptive systems — especially when you don't have enough data to run proper A/B tests?
